About

The name Dimag Me Bhukh means “hunger in the mind”: a hunger to understand nature. It is also written Dimaag Me Bhukh, Dimag Mein Bhukh or Dimag Me Bhookh.

A hunger to understand nature. A commitment to protect it.

At Dimag Me Bhukh, my focus is not simply on installing nests for sparrows. It is on protecting the lives that begin inside those nests.

Providing a nesting space is only the beginning. Once sparrow chicks arrive, they need a safe environment, the right food, clean water, protection from threats, and an understanding of their natural behaviour. If we want sparrows to thrive and become part of our future generations, we must learn how to care for them—not just invite them into our homes.

My name is Akanksha Gupta, and for the past three years, I have had the privilege of living closely alongside sparrows under the same roof. I have observed their lives every day—from the moment they wake up in the morning to the time they settle down for the night.

I have closely observed when they arrive and leave, what they eat, what they need, how they behave, how they raise their young, what works for them, and what can actually harm them. Along the way, I have also explored and observed the truth behind many common myths and facts about sparrows.

Everything I share through Dimag Me Bhukh comes from my personal experiences, observations, and three years of living closely with these beautiful little birds.

My hope is that by sharing what I have learned, I can help more people understand sparrows—not just feed them, but create safer and more meaningful spaces for them to live, breed, and thrive.

Because saving sparrows is not just about giving them a nest.
It is about understanding their lives and protecting their future.

Thank you for being here and for caring about nature.

My 3-Year Sparrow Journey

How living under the same roof as sparrows changed the way I understand them.

  1. How it started

    Three years ago we moved into a house in Greater Noida. At first I did not like it at all. The house was nothing special, there was not even a proper cupboard, and the washrooms were in poor shape. I only stayed because we had no other choice. But I had many plants, and I had loved feeding birds since childhood, so I began keeping grains in my balcony and garden area.

  2. First nest

    One day two sparrows came to eat the grain. Slowly there were more, and then different kinds of birds started to visit. When breeding season was near, the sparrows began carrying twigs to the false ceiling inside my house. The moment they came indoors, we switched our fans off for good. Before long they had built six nests, and six pairs were living in the house.

  3. First chicks

    In one breeding season a single couple raised chicks up to six times, each time at least four and sometimes six. By my count, more than 400 chicks have hatched in my home over three years, and the sparrows kept increasing.

  4. What I learned

    Sparrows stayed and grew in number because they found food, water and safety. With more than 1,000 plants there were plenty of insects to eat. I never used fertiliser or pesticide on my plants, so the sparrows had a natural environment and the insects they found were healthy. A good habitat, not just a nest, is what made them choose my home for breeding.

  5. Discoveries

    I found that sparrows eat cooked food well: poha, vermicelli, dal-rice, khichdi, roti and rice. Broken rice became their favourite grain. I watched their numbers rise day by day, and in three years I did not see them sick or dying. I also found that two common myths, about rice and about touching baby birds, did not hold true in my home. You can read them in Myths vs Facts.

  6. Where I am today

    Humans and birds now live together like a family. Doors and windows stay open day and night so the birds can come and go as they like. Hundreds of sparrows spend the day in my plants, and some sleep there at night. I am sure coexistence is possible, and I want to help more people see it.

What I Have Observed

“These are observations collected from three years of living alongside sparrows—not theories from a distance.”

They eat cooked food too

Besides the dry grains I keep out, my sparrows eat cooked food. I have seen them eat poha, vermicelli, dal-rice and khichdi, and sometimes they take small bites from our plates. It started with my dogs’ food: I cook for them and leave it on the kitchen table to cool, usually boiled rice or roti, and the sparrows began eating it.

Broken rice is their favourite

I have kept bajra, kangni, jowar, daliya and more, but they eat broken rice the most, and the flock keeps growing. About one sack of rice comes into my home every month for the sparrows.

Food that is always there

I keep many kinds of grain out all day and night, and I refill before the bowls are empty. I never let them run out.

Breeding

One couple bred up to six times in a single breeding season, each time with at least four chicks and sometimes six. By my count, more than 400 chicks have hatched in my home in three years.

What the parents feed their chicks

Along with insects, I have personally observed sparrows feeding their chicks and eating soft foods such as cooked rice, soft roti, cooked daliya and even small pieces of dough. As the chicks grow, their diet gradually changes and includes more seeds and grains.

In three years I have watched many broods grow, develop feathers and eventually leave their nests. I have seen chicks grow healthy and strong in an environment where their parents had access to insects, grains and the soft cooked foods I offered.

These foods were part of the feeding environment in which many chicks in my home grew successfully. However, this does not establish that these foods alone meet every chick’s nutritional needs. Insects and other suitable natural prey remain especially important for growing sparrows.

Healthy and growing

In three years I have watched their numbers rise day by day. I have not seen them sick, and I have not seen them dying.

Trust

I never chase or catch them. They sit on lights, photo frames, the TV, wires and indoor plants, play, squabble and chirp all day, and the chirping gives me peace. A few chicks have come very close to my son and to me. We did not disturb them, and we only pick a bird up when it has to be rescued. The trust grew on both sides.

A home outside breeding season too

When breeding season is over, sparrows still use the house as a resting place. Some couples come indoors to eat even when plenty of food is kept outside.

At my dining table

When I sit down to eat, the sparrows know. They come to their own table to eat with me, even when I am only drinking tea. It feels as if their hunger wakes up with mine.

Plants and shelter

One of the biggest changes I made to my home was adding more greenery, both indoors and outdoors. Over time, my collection of plants grew into a small green space that became an important part of my journey with sparrows. I have thorny outdoor plants, bougainvillea, a ber tree, lemon, babul, and bushy ficus. These plants offer different kinds of branches, cover, and places for birds to perch and rest. Dense foliage can also provide shelter from harsh sunlight, wind, and potential predators. My little green space has become a familiar gathering place for sparrows. Hundreds visit throughout the day, and I have observed some settling among the plants in the evening. Watching them move between branches, communicate with one another, and find their preferred spots has helped me appreciate how important vegetation can be to urban birds. I have more than 1,000 plants in my collection, and I have never used chemical fertilisers or pesticides on them. I try to maintain this space naturally, keeping the needs of the birds and the health of the plants in mind. This experience has taught me that creating a bird-friendly home involves much more than putting out food and water. Birds also need greenery, shelter, and safe places to move around. Even a balcony, terrace, or small outdoor space can become more welcoming to wildlife when we choose plants thoughtfully and allow natural cover to develop. My approach is simple: instead of making birds come to us only for food, let us create surroundings where they can find more of what they need to live..

Other birds on my balcony

Although sparrows are at the heart of my three-year journey with birds, they are not the only visitors to my balcony. As I added more plants and made my surroundings greener, I began noticing many other species visiting this little space. Rock chats, Indian robins, bulbuls, pigeons, doves, greater coucals, Indian silverbills, white-eyes, sunbirds, babblers, and black drongos are among the birds I have observed around my balcony. Each species has its own behaviour, calls, feeding habits, and way of interacting with the surroundings. Some birds visit briefly before flying away, while others return regularly. I have enjoyed watching them perch on branches, explore different corners, search for food, and interact with one another. Their visits have made me more curious about the diversity of birdlife that can exist even in a residential neighbourhood. What fascinates me most is that we do not always need a forest or a bird sanctuary to observe wildlife. Sometimes, birds are already living around us, but we fail to notice them because we rarely stop to look. My balcony has taught me to pay attention to these small visitors and appreciate each one without expecting them to behave in a particular way. By adding plants, maintaining a welcoming environment, and allowing birds the space to come and go freely, I have discovered a world of wildlife right outside my window. A balcony may be a small space, but with thoughtful choices, it can become a little window into the natural world.

The lapwing across the road

On the terrace of a house directly opposite mine, three pairs of lapwings (tithari) are nesting. Watching three pairs choose the same terrace to raise their families has been a fascinating experience. Each pair has its own nesting spot, and I have been observing their breeding journey from my home. Lapwings often lay their eggs in open areas, where the eggs can be difficult to spot among the surrounding ground. Unlike birds that nest inside cavities, they rely heavily on vigilance, alarm calls, and protective behaviour to defend their eggs and chicks. The lapwings I observe are remarkably alert and vocal. When they sense a possible threat, they call loudly and remain watchful. Their protective instincts become especially noticeable when their eggs or chicks are nearby. I have observed one female laying four eggs in a clutch and repeating the breeding cycle around three or four times during a season. Yet, despite all this effort, sometimes only one or two chicks survive. It is a reminder that hatching is only the beginning of a much longer struggle for survival. Having three pairs nesting so close to my home has given me a rare opportunity to observe their behaviour over time. It has also reminded me how important it is to respect nesting birds, keep our distance, and avoid disturbing their nests. Sometimes, wildlife does not need us to build a sanctuary. It simply needs us to leave enough space for life to continue.

How to Invite Sparrows

What worked in my home, step by step. Every home is different, so start small and be patient.

  1. Start with grain, every day

    My first two sparrows came for the grain I kept in my balcony. Keep dry grains such as bajra, kangni, jowar, daliya and broken rice. Refill before it runs out and never let it stay empty, so the birds learn that this place is reliable.

  2. Add simple cooked food, in small amounts

    My sparrows happily eat plain boiled rice, roti, poha, dal-rice, khichdi and vermicelli. Offer only fresh, simple food that is not salty, spicy, oily or spoiled, in small amounts, and clear away leftovers.

  3. Keep clean water

    Food, water and safety are what made my sparrows stay. Keep a shallow dish of clean water in a shady spot, and refresh it every day.

  4. Grow shelter

    Thorny and bushy plants make sparrows feel safe. I planted bougainvillea, a ber tree, lemon, babul and bushy ficus, indoors and outdoors. Together they became a mini jungle where hundreds rest all day and some sleep at night.

  5. Keep it chemical-free

    I have never used fertiliser or pesticide on my plants. With many plants and no chemicals, there were healthy insects for the chicks. A natural environment is a big reason sparrows chose to breed here.

  6. Offer a safe place to nest

    When the sparrows began carrying twigs into my false ceiling, I switched the fans off for good, and they built six nests. Offer a sheltered, quiet gap or a nest box, and keep fans off near any active nest.

  7. Be calm and patient

    I have never chased or tried to catch them. They can go wherever they like, and over time they learned that nobody will harm them. Trust takes time. Stay calm, watch from a distance, and let them come to you.

  8. Let them come and go

    My doors and windows stay open day and night so the birds can enter and leave by choice. If you try this, first make sure the space is safe: mark large glass, keep fans off, and keep pets supervised.

  9. Keep everyone safe and clean

    I have three dogs and a young son, and everyone lets the sparrows be. Every home is different: keep pets and children calm around birds, clean droppings regularly, wash your hands after feeding, and keep your own food covered.

This is what worked in my home over three years. It is not a guarantee, and every home and neighbourhood is different.

Sparrow-Friendly Home / Balcony

How to turn your balcony, terrace or garden into a safer space for sparrows.

🪺

Nesting spaces

Sheltered gaps or nest boxes in quiet, shaded, rain-protected spots, away from fans and constant disturbance.

🌾

Food

Simple, suitable food in small amounts in a clean container. Remove leftovers before they spoil.

💧

Water

A shallow dish in the shade, refreshed daily and washed regularly.

🌿

Plants

Dense, leafy, pesticide-free plants and shrubs for cover and insects.

🌙

Roosting spaces

Sheltered branches, ledges or thick shrubs where they can rest and spend the night.

🛡️

Safety

Keep fans off near nests, mark large clear glass, and keep the space clean and quiet.

🐾

Pigeon, cat and dog risks

Cats and dogs can harm chicks and fledglings, so keep them away from nesting areas. Larger birds can crowd feeding spots, so put food near dense cover in small portions.

🚫

Things to avoid

Pesticides, glue traps, loose netting, salty, spicy, oily or spoiled food, and disturbing nests or handling chicks.

In Depth

Daily rhythm, behaviour, food and water, and dangers, with photos from my home.

01

Daily rhythm

Sparrows have a daily routine shaped by daylight, food availability, weather, and the need for safety. In my home, I have observed them becoming active around first light, moving between their familiar spots, searching for food, resting among the plants, and returning to sheltered places as evening approaches. Watching their daily rhythm has helped me understand that a bird-friendly space is not just somewhere birds visit to eat. It can also offer places to perch, rest, shelter, and carry out their everyday activities. Over time, I began noticing familiar patterns: when sparrows arrive, which corners they prefer, where they gather, and how their behaviour changes throughout the day. These small observations have taught me to pay attention to their needs rather than simply counting how many birds visit.

  • Early morning: Notice when sparrows first appear and where they begin their daily activities.
  • During the day: Observe their feeding, resting, bathing, and interactions with other birds.
  • Late afternoon: Watch for changes in activity as daylight fades.
  • Before nightfall: Notice which sheltered spots they choose to settle in, without approaching or disturbing them.

Before nightfall: Notice which sheltered spots they choose to settle in, without approaching or disturbing them. Sometimes, the more carefully we watch, the more we realise how much is happening in the little world just outside our windows.

02

Behaviour and social life

Sparrows are social birds. They feed, rest and bathe in groups, often in dense shrubs, and are noisy and busy around shared spots. Dust bathing and splashing in shallow water are normal.

  • Dense, leafy plants give them cover to gather.
  • Watch from a distance so natural behaviour continues.
03

Food and water

House sparrows are opportunistic eaters. They commonly eat seeds and grains, and also simple household foods and insects, and their diet changes with what is available. Chicks depend heavily on insects in their early days. Fresh water is needed for drinking and bathing.

  • Offer grains and simple foods such as roti, rice, daliya or poha in small amounts.
  • Avoid food that is very salty, spicy, oily, sugary, spoiled or highly processed.
  • Refresh water daily and wash dishes and feeders often.
04

What can harm them

Common household dangers include running ceiling fans, clear glass, cats and other predators, pesticides, and dirty water or feeders. Many can be reduced before nests become active.

  • Keep fans off near active nests.
  • Mark or cover large clear glass.
  • Keep pets away from nesting areas.
  • Avoid pesticides around plants.

Sparrow Babies & Nesting

Protecting chicks is central to everything here. Understand what is happening in the nest.

What happens inside a nest?

Sparrows build a nest of coarse dried vegetation in a sheltered hole or gap, lined with finer material such as feathers, string or paper. Cornell gives a clutch of 1–8 eggs, and incubation takes about 10–14 days. Newly hatched chicks are naked, with closed eyes and bright pink skin, and depend on their parents. Timings vary. Source: Cornell Lab of Ornithology.

What do parents feed their chicks?

House sparrow parents feed their chicks mostly insects and other protein-rich foods in the first days of life, because growing chicks need plenty of protein for healthy development. As the chicks grow, their diet gradually includes more seeds and grains.

This is why a bird-friendly home needs more than a feeder. Green plants, insects, safe nesting spaces, clean water and suitable food all play a role in supporting birds and their young. See What I Have Observed for what I have seen in my own home. Cornell Lab of Ornithology also notes that sparrows feed insects to their young.

When do chicks leave the nest?

One of the most exciting stages in a sparrow’s life is the day a chick finally leaves its nest. After days of hatching, growing, and being fed by its parents, the little bird begins its next stage of life outside the nest. House sparrow chicks typically leave the nest around 14–17 days after hatching, although the exact timing can vary. By this stage, they have grown feathers and developed enough strength to make their first attempts at flight. However, leaving the nest does not mean they are immediately independent. Newly fledged sparrows may look fully feathered but still appear clumsy. Their flight may be weak, their movements uncertain, and they may spend time on the ground or among low branches while building their skills. During this period, their parents continue feeding them and helping them learn how to find food and survive independently. In my three-year journey with sparrows, watching chicks grow from tiny hatchlings into fledglings has been one of the most rewarding experiences. Every stage brings something new to observe, from the parents’ tireless feeding efforts to the first little hops and flights outside the nest. An important thing to remember: a fledgling sitting on the ground is not necessarily abandoned. If it is fully feathered, alert, and uninjured, its parents may still be nearby and caring for it. Keep pets away, observe from a distance, and avoid picking it up unless it is injured or in immediate danger. Nature has its own timeline. Our role is to give young birds the space and safety they need to complete this remarkable journey.

What Should Humans Do, and What Should We Avoid?

When we find a sparrow chick outside its nest, our first instinct may be to pick it up and help. But sometimes, the best way to help is to give the chick space and allow its parents to continue caring for it. Keep pets away from nesting areas, remain quiet, and observe from a safe distance. If possible, maintain clean water sources and natural leafy cover nearby, while ensuring that these do not create additional risks for the birds. Avoid opening or moving an active nest, handling chicks unnecessarily, or disturbing the parents. Keep electric fans and other potential hazards away from nesting areas. Never give chicks milk, and do not force-feed them bread, water, or other food. Young birds have specific nutritional needs, and incorrect feeding can cause serious harm. If a chick is fully feathered, alert, and hopping around, it may simply be a fledgling learning to live outside the nest. If it is injured, weak, cold, or in immediate danger, contact a qualified wildlife rescuer or veterinarian for guidance. The most important lesson I have learned from observing sparrows is that caring for birds does not always mean intervening. Sometimes, responsible care means protecting their space and allowing nature to take its course.

When Does a Sparrow Chick Actually Need Help?

Finding a tiny bird on the ground can be worrying, especially when it appears to be alone. But before intervening, it is important to understand the chick’s age, feather development, and physical condition. Not every chick on the ground is abandoned, and not every chick needs rescuing. Young sparrows go through different stages of development. Nestlings depend heavily on their parents and are usually not ready to survive outside the nest. Fledglings, on the other hand, have most of their feathers and begin exploring the world beyond the nest, even when their flight is still unsteady. During this stage, parent sparrows may continue feeding their young outside the nest. The chick might hop between branches, sit quietly in a sheltered spot, or call out to its parents. If it is alert and uninjured, observing from a distance is often the best first step. Signs that a chick may need help

  • Featherless or barely feathered: A young nestling found outside its nest may need assistance, particularly if the nest is accessible and can be safely restored.
  • Visible injury: Bleeding, a drooping wing, difficulty standing, or an obvious wound requires prompt attention.
  • Weak or unresponsive: A chick that is cold, lethargic, struggling to breathe, or lying still needs urgent assessment.
  • Contact with a cat or dog: Even without visible wounds, a chick caught by a cat may develop a serious infection and needs urgent professional advice.
  • Immediate danger: If the chick is exposed to traffic, predators, or another immediate hazard, carefully move it out of danger if you can do so safely.

    • The most important lesson is to assess before acting. Sometimes a little patience protects a healthy fledgling; at other times, prompt intervention can save a vulnerable bird. Observe carefully, intervene when necessary, and always put the chick’s safety first.

    Myths vs Facts

    Common beliefs about sparrows, and what is closer to the truth.

    Myth

    Does Rice Damage a Sparrow’s Reproductive System?.

    Fact

    IAnother claim I often hear is that eating rice damages a sparrow’s uterus or reproductive system. There is no established scientific evidence that plain rice, when eaten as part of a suitable diet, damages a sparrow’s reproductive organs or directly prevents breeding. In my home, rice is one of the foods sparrows eat most enthusiastically. In fact, it is probably the grain they consume in the largest quantities here. I provide broken rice regularly, yet I have watched sparrows continue to nest, lay eggs, hatch chicks, and raise their young. Over three years, I have counted more than 400 chicks hatching in the natural nests around my home. My observations cannot prove that rice has no possible effects under every circumstance, but they certainly do not support the claim that eating rice automatically damages sparrows’ reproductive systems. What matters is the overall diet. Rice provides energy, but it should not be the only food available. Sparrows also need other nutrients, and growing chicks require protein-rich insects and other suitable natural foods. My observation is simple: the sparrows in my home eat rice in large quantities and continue to breed. We should not confuse a popular belief with an established scientific fact.

    Myth

    If a human touches a baby sparrow, its parents will reject or kill it.

    Fact

    I have rescued baby birds in my home many times, and each time the parents accepted them back. I think this myth exists to stop people from disturbing chicks. Very young, featherless chicks and eggs are more delicate, and touching them could make a chick ill or change an egg, so avoid handling them. I have never touched an egg, and every egg in my home hatched into a healthy chick. Wildlife groups also say that touching does not usually make parents abandon their young.

    Myth

    A baby sparrow found on the ground always needs rescuing.

    Fact

    Finding a baby sparrow on the ground can make us worry that it has fallen from its nest or been abandoned by its parents. But not every young sparrow found on the ground needs rescuing. Understanding its age, appearance, and behaviour is the first step towards deciding what to do. As young sparrows grow, they eventually leave the nest, even when they are not yet strong fliers. These fledglings may hop on the ground, perch on low branches, or sit quietly while learning to navigate their surroundings. Their parents often continue feeding and caring for them outside the nest. If a chick is fully feathered, alert, and able to hop, observe it quietly from a distance. Keep cats, dogs, and other potential threats away, and watch for signs that its parents are nearby. Avoid taking it home simply because it is alone. However, a featherless or barely feathered chick outside the nest may need help. A bird that is injured, bleeding, unusually weak, cold, or unresponsive also requires prompt attention. If it has been caught by a cat, seek urgent advice from a wildlife rescuer or veterinarian, even if no wound is visible. If a healthy fledgling is in immediate danger, move it only as far as necessary to a safer nearby spot, where its parents can still find it. If a nestling needs assistance, seek expert guidance on safely returning it to its nest or arranging appropriate care. My approach is to observe first and intervene when necessary. Helping wildlife does not always mean taking it into our hands; sometimes, the best help is protecting it while its parents continue their care

    Myth

    Sparrows should only be given seeds and grains.

    Fact

    House sparrows are opportunistic eaters. They commonly eat seeds and grains, but they can also eat a variety of foods they find around human homes. Roti, cooked rice, dal-rice, daliya, poha and even vermicelli can be eaten by sparrows. Their diet naturally changes with what is available around them.

    Myth

    Sparrows cannot digest human food.

    Fact

    House sparrows have lived alongside people for centuries and are well adapted to human environments. In everyday life, they may eat small amounts of simple household foods such as roti, rice, daliya, poha or cooked grains. What matters is moderation and avoiding foods that are excessively salty, spicy, oily, sugary, spoiled or highly processed.

    Myth

    Sparrows only eat grain.

    Fact

    Their diet is more diverse than that. Adults eat seeds and grains, while insects and other soft foods can also be important—especially when raising chicks. A bird-friendly environment therefore offers more than a feeder: plants, insects, shelter and nesting spaces all matter.

    Myth

    Putting up a nest is enough to help sparrows.

    Fact

    A nest is only one part of a suitable habitat. Sparrows also need safe places to raise their young, water, natural cover, food sources and protection from hazards. Creating a bird-friendly home means thinking about the whole environment.

    Myth

    Sparrows leave human homes in winter.

    Fact

    House sparrows generally stay close to the places where they live rather than making long seasonal migrations. If a neighbourhood continues to provide suitable shelter, food, water and nesting opportunities, sparrows can remain there throughout the year.

    Myth

    There is one simple reason why sparrows are declining.

    Fact

    There isn’t one universal explanation. Loss of nesting opportunities, changes in urban habitats, reduced insect availability, pesticides and other environmental pressures can all play a role. The situation can also vary from one place to another.

    Myth

    Sparrows cannot live around WiFi, mobile towers and 5G.

    Fact

    One common belief is that sparrows cannot survive near Wi-Fi routers, mobile towers, or 5G networks. Concerns about electromagnetic radiation and its possible effects on birds have led to many discussions, but the scientific evidence does not support the claim that sparrows simply cannot live in such environments. Research has investigated whether radiofrequency electromagnetic fields may affect birds and other wildlife. However, findings remain limited and sometimes inconsistent, and more well-designed research is needed to understand potential effects under different conditions. It would be inaccurate to claim either that all wireless radiation is harmless to birds or that it is responsible for sparrow declines. In my own home, I have observed sparrows nesting, breeding, raising chicks, and returning regularly in a modern residential environment where wireless technology is part of everyday life. These observations show that sparrows can live and breed in such surroundings, but they cannot establish whether electromagnetic exposure has no effects under all circumstances. We also need to consider the other factors that influence sparrow populations. Access to suitable nesting spaces, natural food, insects for growing chicks, clean water, vegetation, and protection from pesticides and other hazards can all contribute to a bird-friendly environment. Rather than attributing every decline in sparrow numbers to a single cause, we should look at the available evidence and the conditions in which birds live. My message is simple: do not accept popular claims without evidence, but do not dismiss scientific questions without investigation either. Sparrows deserve careful research, suitable habitats, and practical conservation efforts based on what we know..

    Myth

    Sparrows need a special diet to live around humans.

    Fact

    Look closely at a sparrow’s real life. They are incredibly adaptable birds. In homes where they have lived alongside people for generations, they may eat whatever simple, suitable food is available—from grains and seeds to roti, rice, daliya, poha and other cooked foods.

    What we should focus on is not “Never let a sparrow eat human food,” but “Don’t make unsuitable processed food their regular diet.”

    This comes from observation at Akanksha’s home. It shows what happens in one particular environment; it is not proof that every human food is safe for every sparrow.

    Each claim on this page is listed here with what the sources say, in our own words, and a link so you can read them yourself. Some sources are from other countries. Indian sources are marked (India).

    Sparrows eat more than seeds and grain.

    Cornell says house sparrows eat mostly grains and seeds, plus livestock feed and, in cities, discarded food, and that in summer they eat insects and feed them to their young. A feeding study of house sparrows in Punjab, India, recorded wheat, pearl millet, rice, other grains, weeds, insects and kitchen scraps in their diet.

    • Cornell Lab of Ornithology: House Sparrow life history
    • Feeding habits of the house sparrow and its nestlings in Punjab (India)

    Simple cooked household food can be part of a sparrow’s diet, in small amounts.

    Cornell lists discarded food among what city sparrows eat, and the Punjab study lists kitchen scraps. A UK farmland guide describes a varied diet. This shows they are flexible eaters. It does not show that every food is healthy, which is why our advice is small amounts of simple food and avoiding salty, spicy, oily, sugary, spoiled or processed items.

    • Cornell Lab of Ornithology: House Sparrow life history
    • Feeding habits of the house sparrow and its nestlings in Punjab (India)
    • GWCT species sheet: House sparrow (UK)

    Chicks depend on insects, so plants and pesticide-free spaces matter.

    Cornell says sparrows feed insects to their young in summer. A wildlife guide describes breeding sparrows catching insects, larvae and caterpillars for their chicks, and lists insecticide use and falling invertebrate numbers among the main threats. India Together makes the same point about pesticides and nestling food.

    • Cornell Lab of Ornithology: House Sparrow life history
    • Asturnatura bird guide: sparrows
    • India Together: were mobile towers responsible for the decline? (India)

    Nesting gaps and cavities matter.

    Cornell says house sparrows nest in holes in buildings and other structures, build nests of coarse dried vegetation lined with feathers, string or paper, and often reuse nests. A European guide lists the loss of cavities in renovated buildings among the threats, and a UK guide says they favour small holes and crevices and will also use nest boxes.

    • Cornell Lab of Ornithology: House Sparrow life history
    • Asturnatura bird guide: sparrows
    • GWCT species sheet: House sparrow (UK)

    There is no single reason for the decline.

    Cornell cites a North American survey showing a cumulative decline of nearly 80% between 1966 and 2019, links recent declines to the industrialisation of farming, and still rates the species as low concern globally. A 2024 study in Gujarat, India, notes that conclusive evidence for any one cause is still lacking, and a wildlife guide lists several pressures together: intensive farming, insecticides, fewer wild seeds, lost cavities, fewer invertebrates and urban pollution.

    • Cornell Lab of Ornithology: House Sparrow life history
    • Journal of Threatened Taxa, 2024: House Sparrow occurrence in Bhavnagar, Gujarat (India)
    • Asturnatura bird guide: sparrows
    • Cornell news: study on House Sparrow decline

    WiFi, mobile towers and 5G are not proven to be the cause.

    Studies in Belgium and Spain found fewer sparrows where signals from mobile phone base stations were stronger. These show a link between two things, not proof of cause. A 2022 study in Guwahati describes the results so far as inconclusive, and India Together says there is no scientific proof in India that towers are a factor. We did not find studies that tested WiFi or 5G on sparrows directly.

    • Everaert & Bauwens 2007: base stations and breeding house sparrows in Belgium
    • Urban Ecosystems 2022: electromagnetic radiation and sparrows in Guwahati (India)
    • India Together: were mobile towers responsible for the decline? (India)

    A baby bird on the ground does not always need rescuing, and touching it does not make its parents abandon it.

    The Wildlife Trusts say fledglings often spend a few days on the ground with their parents nearby, that touched chicks are not abandoned, and that they should be left alone unless in immediate danger. Tufts University’s wildlife clinic advises returning a nestling to its nest if it can be found, and leaving a healthy fledgling alone. For an injured bird, contact a local wildlife rescue.

    • The Wildlife Trusts (UK): what to do if you find a baby bird
    • Tufts Wildlife Clinic: how to help a baby bird found on the ground

    Rescue & Help

    Found an injured or baby bird? Stay calm, observe first, then decide.

    When to step in

    The bird is injured, bleeding or has a drooping wing, was attacked by a cat or dog, is in danger such as on a road, is a featherless chick out of its nest, or is cold, weak and lying still.

    When not to interfere

    A fully feathered, hopping and alert fledgling with parents nearby usually does not need rescuing. Watch from a distance for a few hours and keep pets away.

    Keeping an injured bird safe

    Place it in a small cardboard box with air holes and a soft cloth, in a warm, dark, quiet room away from pets and children. Handle it as little as possible. Do not force food or water.

    Finding help

    Contact a local wildlife rescue group, bird rescue volunteers or an avian or wildlife vet. In many places a state forest department wildlife helpline is also available.

    Why proper treatment matters

    Birds hide illness and can decline quickly. Wrong food, water or handling can cause stress or harm. Trained rehabilitators know the right care, medicines and safe release.

    Stories from My Sparrow Family

    Moments from everyday life with the sparrows who share my home.

    🪺

    When the fans went off

    When the sparrows started carrying twigs to the false ceiling inside my house, I knew what I had to do: we switched our fans off for good. Slowly, one pair after another built nests. Before long there were six nests and six pairs living inside our home.

    🍵

    At my dining table

    Whenever I sit down to eat, the sparrows seem to know. They arrive at their own table to eat with me, and even when I am only sipping tea, they start eating. It feels as if their hunger wakes up with mine. I love having them with me.

    🐕

    Dogs, a child and sparrows

    We have three dogs and a young son at home, and nobody disturbs the sparrows. It began with the dogs’ food. I cook for them and put it on the kitchen table to cool, and the sparrows started eating it. Today they live with us like family.

    🥚

    The mother across the road

    On a terrace right in front of our house, a lapwing lays four eggs each time, around three or four times in a season. I have watched her life too. She is a fearless mother, loud day and night, and fiercely protective of her chicks.
